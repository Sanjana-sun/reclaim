How to appeal a denied claim in 5 steps

A denial is the start of a negotiation, not the end. Here's the exact sequence that turns a "no" into a "covered."

1. Read the denial letter — find the reason code

Every denial states why. It's usually one of a handful of reasons: not medically necessary, no prior authorization, experimental/investigational, step therapy required, out of network, or a coding error. The reason dictates your entire argument, so pin it down first. Also note the claim number, date of service, and the exact amount denied.

2. Find your deadline — and calendar it today

Appeal windows are strict and vary by plan: commercial and ACA plans generally give you 180 days from the denial to file an internal appeal; Medicare Advantage is often 65 days for a reconsideration. Miss it and you usually lose the right to appeal, no matter how strong your case. Write the date down before you do anything else.

3. Gather your evidence — especially from your doctor

The single most powerful attachment is a letter of medical necessity from your treating physician, plus the relevant chart notes, test results, and any published clinical guidelines your doctor relies on. If the denial was a coding or billing error, get the itemized bill. Your evidence should directly rebut the stated reason.

4. Write the appeal — clear, factual, firm

A good appeal: identifies you and the claim; states plainly that you're appealing and want the denial overturned; makes the reason-specific argument (for medical necessity, it references your physician's judgment — not your own); lists the attachments; and requests a written decision within the plan's timeframe. Keep it professional and specific. Don't over-claim, and never rely on guarantees.

5. Send it the right way — and track it

Submit through the plan's stated channel and keep proof (certified mail or a portal confirmation). If the internal appeal fails, most plans owe you an external review by an independent party — a second, often better, shot. Track every date.

Educational content only — not legal or medical advice. Deadlines and rights vary by plan and state; check your plan documents.
